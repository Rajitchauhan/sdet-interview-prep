## What this course is trying to teach

The course is not simply asking you to add QA keywords to a resume or
memorise interview answers. It is teaching you to make your work
**visible, understandable and credible**:

1.  A recruiter should quickly understand what QA work you have actually
    done.
2.  A hiring manager should be able to inspect your project and
    understand its purpose.
3.  An interviewer should hear how you think, investigate, communicate
    and make trade-offs.
4.  Your examples should be honest and supported by evidence.

# Module 1 --- Resume and public QA portfolio

## 1.1 Think like a recruiter

The course describes a hiring process in which a resume may first pass
through an Applicant Tracking System (ATS), then receive a short human
scan. The transcript uses an illustrative estimate of about six seconds
for an initial recruiter scan. Treat that as a teaching illustration,
not a universal measured rule for every employer.

### What an ATS does

An ATS helps an employer organise and search applications. The course
explains that systems may use keyword and contextual matching to connect
a candidate's experience with a job description.

**Practical implication:** write clearly and use the exact tool and
skill names that accurately describe your experience. Do not expect a
recruiter or ATS to infer that "browser automation" means Playwright if
you have genuinely used Playwright and can name it.

### Keywords highlighted by the course

-   Playwright
-   Postman
-   Jira
-   End-to-end testing
-   Manual testing
-   Test case design
-   Defect documentation
-   Exploratory testing

The course's sample also includes Zephyr, BrowserStack and SQL.

**Important:** only list a tool if you have actually used it and can
explain what you did with it. Keyword matching is not a reason to
exaggerate.

## 1.2 A strong resume bullet formula

The activity uses:

**Action Verb + Task + Measurable Result**

A good bullet tells the reader:

-   **What you did:** a strong action verb.
-   **What work you performed:** the feature, workflow or testing
    activity.
-   **Evidence or scope:** a real count, percentage, browser, endpoint,
    journey or outcome.

Useful action verbs from the course:

`Authored`, `Designed`, `Executed`, `Validated`, `Identified`,
`Automated`, `Maintained`, `Configured`, `Queried`, `Documented`,
`Escalated`, `Coordinated`.

Avoid vague openings such as:

-   Helped with testing.
-   Worked on automation.
-   Was responsible for QA.
-   Tested web applications.

### Course-style example

Weak:

> Tested web applications using Playwright.

Stronger:

> Authored 15 end-to-end test cases using Playwright, covering three
> core user journeys across Chrome and Firefox.

The stronger example names the tool, scope and evidence. Use your own
actual numbers; do not copy sample figures as if they were your results.

## 1.3 Resume activity --- step by step

The course activity asks the learner to create a targeted Projects or
Experience section for Junior QA, QA Analyst or Test Automation roles.

### Step 1 --- Select a real target job posting

Find one real job advertisement and record:

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  What to inspect                     Questions
  ----------------------------------- -----------------------------------
  Tools                               Does it mention Playwright,
                                      Selenium, Cypress, Jira, Postman,
                                      SQL or another tool?

  Responsibilities                    Does it emphasise test cases,
                                      regression, API testing,
                                      exploratory testing or defect
                                      tracking?

  Soft skills                         Does it mention communication,
                                      attention to detail or
                                      collaboration?

  Role level                          Is it genuinely suitable for your
                                      experience level?
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

Keep the job description open while writing. Tailor your resume to that
role rather than making one generic version for every vacancy.

### Step 2 --- Study the sample resume

The uploaded sample uses two course projects:

**MindBridge Education --- Manual QA Tester** - Web-based learning
platform. - Manual and exploratory testing. - Test cases, defects and
results documented using Jira and Zephyr.

**GreenLeaf Grocers --- QA Automation Tester** - E-commerce platform. -
Playwright automation for checkout and product search. - Postman API
validation. - BrowserStack cross-browser testing. - SQL queries for
backend data verification.

These are course scenarios. Do not present them as your own work unless
you personally completed those projects and can defend the details.

### Step 3 --- Inventory your complete experience

Create two lists.

**QA project evidence** - Number of test cases authored. - Number and
type of defects found. - Number of automation scripts created. - Tools
used for each activity. - User journeys and browsers covered. - API
endpoints validated. - Database checks performed. - Regression cycles
completed.

**Prior experience and transferable skills** - Following a defined
process. - Finding and correcting errors. - Documenting issues. -
Communicating with clients or colleagues. - Troubleshooting. - Working
with data. - Meeting deadlines. - Maintaining accuracy.

The course stresses that previous experience outside QA can still be
relevant. For example, customer support can demonstrate complaint
documentation and communication; retail or operations can demonstrate
process adherence and data accuracy.

### Step 4 --- Draft at least four bullets

The activity asks for at least four bullets in total: - At least three
about QA project work. - At least one about a prior role or other
experience, if applicable. - If there is no prior experience, use four
or more QA project bullets.

Each QA bullet should: 1. Start with an action verb. 2. Name at least
one relevant tool explicitly. 3. Include a real number or measurable
scope. 4. Describe actual work from the project.

### Step 5 --- Tailor to the job description

Ask yourself:

-   Does the employer specifically ask for Jira, while my bullet only
    says "issue tracker"?
-   Does the job emphasise regression testing, but my resume does not
    mention it even though I performed it?
-   Can I accurately incorporate the employer's terminology?
-   Am I claiming experience I cannot explain?

Revise at least two bullets to better match the target role, without
misrepresenting your work.

### Step 6 --- Quality-check every bullet

-   [ ] Starts with a strong action verb.
-   [ ] Names the tool used, where relevant.
-   [ ] Includes a real number, percentage or measurable scope.
-   [ ] Reflects the target job's priorities.
-   [ ] Describes actual work.
-   [ ] Avoids vague phrases such as "helped with" or "worked on".

### Step 7 --- Format the section

Use clear headings and a short context line.

Example structure:

``` text
PROJECTS

Project Name | QA Automation Tester — Course/Portfolio Project
One-line description of the application and scope.

• Authored ...
• Validated ...
• Documented ...
```

The course activity asks learners to export and submit the section as a
PDF. For your own job search, keep an editable source document too, and
export a clean PDF when applying if the employer accepts PDF.

## 1.5 Public GitHub portfolio

The course's portfolio lesson makes a key distinction:

-   **Code repository:** code exists, but the purpose and context may be
    unclear.
-   **Portfolio repository:** the work is organised and explained so a
    recruiter can understand what you built and why.

The transcript says recruiters may ask three quick questions:

1.  What did you build?
2.  Does it look professional?
3.  Can I understand it without technical knowledge?

### Profile checklist

-   Clear profile title or bio.
-   Relevant QA projects pinned near the top.
-   Short, descriptive repository names.
-   One-line repository descriptions that identify tools and purpose.
-   Professional README files.
-   No unexplained collection of generic repositories.

The course transcript states GitHub allows up to six pinned
repositories. Platform features can change, so check GitHub's current
interface when configuring your profile.

### README: the cover letter for your code

The course identifies four core README components:

#### 1. Project Overview

One short paragraph explaining: - What the application does. - What you
tested. - Why those workflows matter.

#### 2. Tech Stack

List the actual tools and technologies used, for example: - Playwright -
JavaScript - Node.js - VS Code

For a Python-based project, list the real Python, Pytest, Playwright and
supporting tools used.

#### 3. Test Coverage

Explain: - How many test cases exist. - Which user journeys are
covered. - Which browsers or environments were tested. - Which types of
tests are included.

Use real figures from your own work. The transcript explicitly warns
against fabricated business-impact numbers.

#### 4. Setup Instructions

Provide simple, numbered steps so another person can run the tests
locally. Include prerequisites, installation, configuration and test
commands where appropriate.

### Recruiter-readability test

The course suggests checking whether a non-technical person can
understand what you built in under 30 seconds. If not, improve the
overview and README structure.

### Mentor extension --- recommended QA repository structure

This is an implementation suggestion, not a verbatim course requirement:

``` text
qa-playwright-project/
├── README.md
├── requirements.txt
├── pytest.ini
├── .gitignore
├── config/
├── tests/
│   ├── ui/
│   └── api/
├── pages/
├── fixtures/
├── test_data/
├── utils/
├── reports/
└── docs/
```

Do not create empty folders merely to make a project look large. Add
structure when the project genuinely needs it.

# Module 2 --- QA interview thinking and behavioural answers

## 2.1 What is the interviewer actually evaluating?

The course argues that interviewers are not only checking whether you
can define a test case. Behavioural questions about failure, bugs,
deadlines and disagreements help them understand how you think under
pressure.

The QA role sits between delivery speed and product quality: -
Developers and product teams want to deliver features. - QA must
evaluate whether the product is sufficiently safe and reliable to
release. - QA needs judgment, communication and confidence to raise
risks professionally.

## 2.2 Quality advocacy mindset

The course calls this **quality advocacy**: commitment to the end user's
experience, expressed through day-to-day decisions.

It means: - Speaking up when behaviour appears incorrect. - Explaining
risk to non-technical stakeholders without unnecessary jargon. -
Discussing defects professionally when a developer disagrees. -
Balancing urgency, user impact and release constraints. - Supporting
decisions with evidence rather than emotion.

A QA engineer is not there to "win" arguments with developers. The goal
is to help the team make an informed quality decision.

## 2.3 STAR method for behavioural answers

STAR is the answer structure in the course:

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Letter                  Meaning                 What to say
  ----------------------- ----------------------- -----------------------
  S                       Situation               Give the context. What
                                                  was happening?

  T                       Task                    What were you
                                                  responsible for?

  A                       Action                  Explain exactly what
                                                  you did. This is the
                                                  most important part.

  R                       Result                  Explain the outcome.
                                                  Quantify it when you
                                                  can do so honestly.
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

### Course example: critical defect before release

**Question:** "Tell me about a time you caught a critical defect before
release."

-   **Situation:** During the final regression cycle for the GreenLeaf
    Grocers checkout flow.
-   **Task:** Responsible for validating the payment confirmation screen
    across three browsers.
-   **Action:** Ran the Playwright automation suite, noticed a
    consistent Firefox failure on the order summary page, investigated
    it, confirmed a rendering defect affecting the total price display,
    and logged a Priority 1 defect in Jira with reproduction steps and
    screenshots.
-   **Result:** The defect was fixed and retested before the release
    window closed, preventing a customer-facing pricing error.

**Source distinction:** This is a course scenario. Use it as a model for
structuring an answer, not as a personal achievement unless you actually
performed that work.

### How to make STAR answers stronger

-   Keep Situation and Task concise.
-   Spend most of the answer on Action.
-   Explain your own contribution, not only what "the team" did.
-   Give a specific result.
-   If there was no measurable result, state the verified outcome
    without inventing a percentage.

## 2.4 Question: "How do you write a bug report?"

The simulated interview gives a practical sequence.

### Strong process

1.  Reproduce the defect consistently.
2.  Confirm the environment.
3.  Open the defect in a tracking tool such as Jira.
4.  Write a specific title.
5.  Record environment and preconditions.
6.  Add clear steps to reproduce.
7.  Record actual result.
8.  Record expected result.
9.  Attach useful evidence such as screenshots or a screen recording.
10. Assign severity based on user and business impact.

### Course example

Weak title:

> Button broken.

More useful title:

> Checkout button fails to submit form when the postcode field is empty
> on Chrome.

Example defect: - User leaves postcode empty. - User selects Submit. -
Page refreshes silently. - No validation message or useful feedback
appears. - User cannot understand how to proceed.

The candidate documents the defect in Jira, adds exact reproduction
steps and a screen recording, and classifies it as a major defect
because it blocks order completion.

### Interview-ready answer pattern

> "First, I reproduce the issue to make sure it is consistent. Then I
> capture the environment and write a clear title, preconditions, steps
> to reproduce, actual result and expected result in Jira. I attach
> evidence such as a screenshot or recording and assess severity based
> on user impact. For example, if submitting checkout with a required
> field empty silently refreshes the page and blocks the order, I would
> document that exact flow and flag the impact clearly."

Adapt the tool name to the tool you genuinely use.

## 2.5 Question: "How would you prioritise two bugs?"

Course scenario: - Bug A: checkout flow is completely blocked. - Bug B:
small UI misalignment on the confirmation page.

The candidate prioritises the checkout blocker because users cannot
complete a purchase. The UI issue is still logged, but may not block
release depending on product risk and the team's release criteria.

### Important distinction

-   **Severity:** How serious is the defect's impact?
-   **Priority:** How urgently should the team address it?

The course dialogue focuses on severity and release impact. In a real
team, severity and priority should be discussed separately; a
high-severity defect is not automatically assigned the same priority in
every context. Product impact, frequency, workaround, release timing and
stakeholder decisions can influence priority.

### Interview answer pattern

> "I would first assess user impact and whether a critical journey is
> blocked. A checkout blocker would normally receive higher urgency
> because users cannot complete a purchase. I would log both defects
> with evidence, explain the impact and discuss release risk with the
> team. The visual issue should still be tracked, but it may not justify
> blocking the release if it does not affect the core journey and the
> product owner accepts the risk."

## 2.6 Question: "What if a developer says your bug isn't a bug?"

The course reading recommends evidence and collaboration, not backing
down immediately or escalating aggressively.

### Four-step approach

1.  **Revisit evidence:** Recheck environment, reproduction steps,
    actual and expected behaviour.
2.  **Align with requirements:** Compare the behaviour with acceptance
    criteria. If requirements are unclear, raise that ambiguity.
3.  **Discuss calmly:** Ask the developer to explain their
    interpretation. If needed, involve the product owner or business
    analyst to clarify expected behaviour.
4.  **Document the outcome:** Record whether the issue is accepted,
    rejected or deferred, with the reasoning.

### Interview-ready answer

> "I would first recheck the evidence and make sure the defect is
> reproducible. Then I would compare the actual behaviour with the
> acceptance criteria and discuss the difference with the developer
> using facts, not opinions. If the requirement is ambiguous, I would
> ask the product owner or business analyst to clarify it. Finally, I
> would document the decision and its reasoning in the tracking tool."

This shows that QA is collaborative, not adversarial.

## 2.7 Question: "When would you NOT automate a test?"

The course explicitly says that "automate everything" is not a mature
strategy.

### Examples that may remain manual

-   Exploratory testing: the purpose is to discover unexpected
    behaviour.
-   One-time or low-frequency checks: script creation and maintenance
    may cost more than the time saved.
-   Human-judgment activities: usability, subjective visual design and
    some accessibility experience evaluations.
-   Unstable features: frequent changes may make scripts brittle before
    they provide value.

### Core principle

**Automation is an investment.** Consider execution frequency,
maintenance cost, stability, repeatability and the value of faster
feedback.

### Interview-ready answer

> "I would not automate every test. Exploratory testing often needs
> human observation because the goal is to discover unexpected
> behaviour. One-time tests may not justify the development and
> maintenance cost of automation. I would also avoid automating a
> rapidly changing feature until its behaviour stabilises. I would
> prioritise repeatable, high-value regression checks where automation
> provides reliable feedback over time."

Nuance: automation can support accessibility and visual checks, but it
does not fully replace human evaluation. The decision depends on the
specific check.

## 2.8 How to answer when you have no professional QA experience

The course says a candidate can use course projects if they can
explain: - What they tested. - Why they chose that approach. - What they
found. - How they documented it. - What they learned.

Do not imply that a course project was commercial production work. Label
it clearly as a course, lab, portfolio or capstone project.

A credible project answer is better than a vague claim about
professional experience you do not have.

## 2.9 Translating previous experience into QA strengths

Do not apologise for changing careers. Explain the transferable skill
honestly.

The course gives a customer-support example: handling checkout
complaints can help someone understand user frustration and edge cases.

Possible transferable areas: - Customer communication → explaining
defects and user impact. - Process adherence → following test
procedures. - Data entry/verification → data accuracy and validation. -
Troubleshooting → structured investigation. - Documentation →
reproducible defect reports. - Deadline management → planning and
prioritising testing.

Use only examples that genuinely describe your own work.

## Exercise 4 --- Bug report drill

Use this scenario:

A user enters a valid email and password, selects Login, and the page
remains on the login screen without any message. The browser console
shows no obvious error.

Write: 1. A precise bug title. 2. Environment. 3. Preconditions. 4.
Steps to reproduce. 5. Expected result. 6. Actual result. 7. Evidence
you would collect. 8. Questions you would ask before assigning severity.

Do not assume the root cause from the UI symptom alone.

## Exercise 5 --- Prioritisation drill

A release candidate has: - A payment submission blocker affecting all
users. - A typo on a low-visibility settings page. - A password reset
email delayed by 30 seconds. - A flaky test that fails intermittently in
CI.

For each item, state: - User/business impact. - Severity estimate. -
Suggested priority and why. - Whether it blocks release. - What evidence
or stakeholder input is still needed.

There may be more than one defensible answer. Explain your reasoning.

you had to investigate an unclear issue. 3. A disagreement or different
interpretation. 4. A mistake or missed detail. 5. A deadline or
competing priorities. 6. A time you communicated technical information
simply. 7. A decision about whether automation was worthwhile.

If you have no professional QA story, use a clearly labelled project or
lab example.

# Interview answer framework and recovery when your mind goes blank

## Technical questions: D-E-A-R

Use this mentor-created structure:

-   **D --- Define:** Give a direct definition.
-   **E --- Explain:** Explain how it works or why it matters.
-   **A --- Apply:** Give a practical example.
-   **R --- Risks/trade-offs:** Mention a limitation or decision factor.

Example for "What is regression testing?": 1. Define it. 2. Explain that
it checks existing functionality after a change. 3. Give an example:
after modifying checkout, rerun relevant existing purchase tests. 4.
Mention scope selection based on impact and risk.

## Behavioural questions: STAR

Use Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Action section strongest.

## If your mind goes blank

1.  Pause for two or three seconds. A short pause is better than
    panicked guessing.
2.  Repeat or reframe the question to confirm what is being asked.
3.  Start with the principle you do remember.
4.  Walk through a logical process.
5.  Use a small example.
6.  Be transparent about what you have not used in production.
7.  If you do not know, explain how you would investigate safely.

Useful phrases:

-   "Let me structure my answer in two parts."
-   "I want to make sure I understood the scenario correctly."
-   "I haven't used that exact tool in a production environment, but the
    underlying concept is..."
-   "I would verify this against the requirement and then reproduce the
    behaviour."
-   "I don't want to guess the exact detail. I can explain how I would
    investigate it."
-   "The key trade-off here is reliability versus maintenance effort."

Do not say "I know everything" or invent an answer. Interviewers often
value a clear, honest reasoning process more than confident guessing.
